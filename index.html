Step 2: Could the hospital write some of this off?

Nonprofit hospitals must have a financial assistance policy. Many patients who qualify never apply. This check uses the 2026 federal poverty guidelines for the 48 contiguous states and DC. Each hospital sets its own income limits, often between 200 and 300 percent of the poverty level, so treat this as a strong signal, not a guarantee.

Step 5: Good Faith Estimate checker

For patients with no insurance, or who chose to pay for themselves. If your final bill from a provider is at least $400 more than that provider's written Good Faith Estimate, federal rules generally let you dispute the bill through the patient provider dispute process, within 120 days of the first bill. Keep both documents, they are the whole case.

Step 6: If you hit a wall

Three endgames for when the first moves do not work, plus the free human help that exists for each one. DueEasy cannot file anything for you, so this step hands you the exact next door to knock on.

Your appeal was denied again: ask for external review

After a final internal denial, most private and Marketplace plan members can ask for external review: an independent reviewer who does not work for your plan reads the case, and the decision binds the plan. In a four state study of these reviews, about 46 percent of denials were overturned. Almost nobody files, mostly because nobody tells them the right exists. Under the federal standard you have 4 months from the final denial notice to request it, and some states set shorter windows.

How to file and what to send: HealthCare.gov external review guide. Send the final denial letter, your internal appeal and everything you attached to it, and your medical records for the service. If your case is a hard medical necessity fight, a free human case manager may beat any template: the Patient Advocate Foundation, 1-800-532-5274, takes denial cases for people with serious or chronic conditions.

Stuck because you only have a summary bill: request the itemized bill

Steps 1 and 4 need the itemized bill with codes, and most people are only ever mailed a summary. You have the right to the full itemized statement. Generate the request, then send it and keep proof.

The bill is valid but you cannot pay it: negotiate it

If you owe the money and assistance does not apply, the number is still often negotiable. Advocates routinely see 30 to 40 percent off for a lump sum paid at once. Rules: never agree on the first call, ask for the hospital's own zero interest payment plan instead of a medical credit card, and get any agreement in writing before you pay a cent.

Free human help, by problem

  • Hospital financial assistance paperwork: Dollar For, a free nonprofit, will fill in the hospital application, submit it, and follow up for you after a short screening.
  • Medication copay you cannot afford: TotalAssist runs grants for copays, premiums, and day of treatment charges across many conditions.
  • Insurer ignoring deadlines or acting in bad faith: file a free complaint with your state insurance department through the NAIC directory. Job based plan problems can also go to the U.S. Department of Labor benefits advisors at 1-866-444-3272.
  • Surprise bill nobody will fix: the No Surprises Help Desk, 1-800-985-3059, or cms.gov/nosurprises.

What DueEasy checks, in plain terms

A denied claim is not final. Most denials can be appealed, and the deadline depends on your plan type: 180 days for most private and Marketplace plans, 60 for Medicare Advantage, 120 for Original Medicare. DueEasy calculates your date and drafts the appeal letter. Read the appeal guide.

A hospital bill may qualify for financial assistance. Nonprofit hospitals must run assistance programs, free care is common at or below 200 percent of the federal poverty level, and you generally have 240 days from the first bill to apply. The screener shows where your household lands. Read the assistance guide.

The bill and the EOB should agree. When they do not, the gap is usually a duplicate line or a number your insurer already said you do not owe. The matcher flags both and totals the difference. Read the matching guide.

A self pay bill far above its estimate can be disputed. If the bill beats your Good Faith Estimate by $400 or more, a federal dispute can be opened within 120 days. Read the dispute guide.

Surprise bills have their own rules. The No Surprises Act caps many emergency and out of network charges at your in network rate, with one big exception: ground ambulances. Read the surprise bill guide.

What is left can often be negotiated. After errors and assistance, a specific reduction request works better than a payment plan at the full amount. Read the negotiation guide.
