How this works

1. Triage.
Answer five questions. Get the right first move for your situation, in the right order.
2. Assistance check.
See if your income may qualify you for hospital financial assistance before you pay anything.
3. Appeal builder.
Enter the facts from your denial letter. Get a finished appeal letter, a records checklist, and your deadline.
4. Bill vs EOB matcher.
Line up every charge on the bill against the EOB. Flags duplicates and amounts that do not match, then drafts the call script.
5. Estimate checker.
No insurance, or paying yourself? Compare your final bill to your Good Faith Estimate and see if you can dispute the gap.

This tool gives general information and document help. It is not legal advice and not medical advice. Your denial letter and your plan documents control your exact rights and deadlines.

Step 2: Could the hospital write some of this off?

Nonprofit hospitals must have a financial assistance policy. Many patients who qualify never apply. This check uses the 2026 federal poverty guidelines for the 48 contiguous states and DC. Each hospital sets its own income limits, often between 200 and 300 percent of the poverty level, so treat this as a strong signal, not a guarantee.

Step 3: Build your appeal

Copy the facts from your denial letter. For most private plans you have 180 days from the denial notice to file an internal appeal, but your letter is the source that counts, so enter the deadline your letter gives you if it lists one.

Your denial letter and your state department of insurance can tell you how to file. The No Surprises Help Desk is 1-800-985-3059.

Step 4: Bill vs EOB matcher

Add one row for each line on the bill. The EOB amount is what your insurance says you owe for that line. If the bill asks for more, that difference is your first dispute. Rows with the same code, same date, and same billed amount get flagged as possible duplicates.

Step 5: Good Faith Estimate checker

For patients with no insurance, or who chose to pay for themselves. If your final bill from a provider is at least $400 more than that provider's written Good Faith Estimate, federal rules generally let you dispute the bill through the patient provider dispute process, within 120 days of the first bill. Keep both documents, they are the whole case.
